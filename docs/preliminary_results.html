1Question and motivation

Has the labour-supply cost of an additional child changed between 1980, in the middle of the post-war climb, and the 2020s, on the plateau? Event-study "child penalty" estimates cannot separate the effect of an extra child from the timing of the first. The sex mix of the first two children can: parents of two same-sex children are 6–7 percentage points more likely to have a third, and sex at birth is as good as random.

For mother i in period t, with Z = 1 if the first two children share a sex, the first stage is morekids = πtZ + Xγ + v and the structural equation is y = βt·morekids + Xδ + u, with X = age, age squared, age at first birth where available, sex of the first child, and race/ethnicity. βt is the local average treatment effect for compliers, and the paper's object is β2020s − β1980.

In 1980 two girls raise the chance of a third child more than two boys, the son-preference asymmetry Angrist and Evans documented. Shaded rows are the Angrist–Evans replication: their published married-women estimates are −0.120 for employment and −5.66 weeks with the two-instrument specification.

  • Replication. OLS and 2SLS coincide for the extensive margin in 1980, so selection into fertility barely biases OLS there. For Black and Hispanic mothers the IV effect on labour income is zero while OLS is strongly negative.
  • The modern 2SLS is uninformative. Point estimates are positive with standard errors of 0.26–0.41; the minimum detectable effect on employment is 0.73. OLS in the 2020s (−0.107) resembles 1980 OLS, but OLS is not the causal parameter.
  • Overidentification rejects in 1980. Two boys and two girls give different LATEs, taken up next.

Mothers induced to have a third child by two boys withdraw from work three times as much as those induced by two girls. Both instruments are randomly assigned, so either the two complier groups have different treatment effects, or sex composition affects labour supply directly and the exclusion restriction fails. The published same-sex LATE is a weighted average of the two.

In the Black/Hispanic subsample the two LATEs are similar (−0.21 vs −0.16, p = 0.78). Which interpretation holds is a central question for the paper: the ACS lets the test be repeated by decade, and fathers' outcomes serve as a placebo for direct effects.

6Where the effect bites

The effect is about −0.13 at every threshold up to 40 weeks and shrinks toward −0.07 at full-year work. A third child moves mothers from any work to no work rather than trimming weeks among those who keep working, so the extensive margin is the headline outcome.

9Instrument validity

The Kitagawa (2015) inequalities, which any valid binary instrument must satisfy for every outcome set, hold in all twelve treatment-by-weeks-bin cells: every difference is positive with t-statistics between 4 and 23, and the bootstrap p-value for a violation is 1.00. The test has power against gross exclusion failures but not against the two-boys/two-girls discrepancy, which concerns the two instruments jointly.

The interaction test flags a difference, but it is driven by an imprecise and implausibly large positive modern estimate. The honest reading is that the CPS sample establishes the first stage and nothing about the LATE. The ACS 1-year files hold roughly 70,000 target mothers per year; pooled over 2005–2023 the standard error on the modern LATE would be near 0.013.

11What would make this a paper

  • Full-power LATEs by decade, 1980–2023, from the IPUMS extract already scripted.
  • Resolve the two-boys/two-girls discrepancy: repeat it in every decade, add fathers' labour supply and household earnings, and read it through the Mogstad–Torgovitsky–Walters framework for 2SLS with multiple instruments.
  • Add the twins-at-second-birth instrument as a second complier population.
  • Interact the LATE with state childcare costs and paid-leave adoption in the ACS years, turning the design into a test of policy moderators.
